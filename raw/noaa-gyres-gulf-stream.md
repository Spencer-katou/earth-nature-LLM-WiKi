# NOAA — Gyres, boundary currents, Gulf Stream (notes)

## Gyres
- A gyre is a large system of rotating ocean currents; named currents (e.g., Gulf Stream) make them up.
- Five major gyres: North Pacific, South Pacific, North Atlantic, South Atlantic, Indian Ocean subtropical gyres.
- Formation: global winds drag surface water; Coriolis effect deflects currents right (clockwise) in the NH and left (counter-clockwise) in the SH. No gyres at the equator (no Coriolis effect).
- "Gyre" is increasingly used for plastic/debris accumulations, but traditionally means rotating currents.

## Boundary currents
- Each gyre has a strong, narrow western boundary current and a weak, broad eastern boundary current.
- Gulf Stream = western boundary current of the North Atlantic gyre; Canary Current = its eastern boundary current.

## Ekman spiral
- Theorised by Vagn Walfrid Ekman (1874–1954) in 1902. Wind drags surface water; each deeper layer moves slower and is deflected by Coriolis; motion ceases at ~100 m; deeper layers can flow opposite to the surface.

## Gulf Stream
- Average speed 6.4 km/h (4 mph); fastest near surface, max ~9 km/h (5.6 mph); slows to ~1.6 km/h (1 mph) as it widens northward. Tutorial gives 25–75 miles/day (≈1–3 knots, 1.85–5.55 km/h).
- Transports more water than all the world's rivers combined.
- Path: from the Gulf of Mexico, through the Strait of Florida, north along Florida, turns east off North Carolina, northeast across the Atlantic; continues as the North Atlantic Drift.
- Keeps Florida's east coast milder; warms western Europe. Main conveyor of heat from south to north in the Atlantic.
- Width, depth, Sverdrup transport not stated.
