# NSIDC — Ice sheets (notes)

## General
- Ice sheet = glacial land ice covering >50,000 km². Today only two: Greenland and Antarctica. During Pleistocene ice ages ice sheets covered much of the Northern Hemisphere.
- Together they hold >99% of Earth's land (freshwater) ice and >68% of Earth's fresh water. Called "global water towers".
- Ice caps: <50,000 km²; largest is on Severny Island, Novaya Zemlya (~20,500 km²). Others: Vatnajökull, Austfonna.

## Antarctic Ice Sheet
- Area almost 14 million km² (≈ contiguous US + Mexico). Volume ~30 million km³. Max thickness nearly 4.9 km.
- Full melt → sea level rise ~58 m.
- Three parts: East Antarctic Ice Sheet (largest; summit 3,000–4,000 m), West Antarctic Ice Sheet (about Greenland-sized; bed largely below sea level; highest parts ~2,500 m), Antarctic Peninsula (smallest).
- Floating ice shelves (where outlet glaciers/ice streams lose contact with bed) are most common in Antarctica.
- Antarctica is losing ice, but not yet as quickly as Greenland or other glaciers.

## Greenland Ice Sheet
- Area ~1.7 million km² (~80% of Greenland; ~3× Texas). Volume ~2.9 million km³. Max thickness >3 km. Summit 3,000–4,000 m.
- Full melt → ~7.4 m sea level rise (feet figure inconsistent on pages: 23 vs 24 ft).
- Sermeq Kujalleq (Jakobshavn Isbræ) reached ~15 km/yr in the 21st century.
- Has lost mass every year since 1998; substantial loss in 21st century from human-caused warming. Research suggests entire sheet could melt by year 3000 under high emissions.

## Mass balance & flow
- Gain: snowfall. Loss: melting, iceberg calving, evaporation (sublimation). Balanced if gains = losses; measured over a full year.
- Flow: cm to a few m/yr near summit; hundreds of m to several km/yr in coastal outlet glaciers; ice streams are fast ribbons.
- Ocean heat melts undersides of ice shelves and tidewater glaciers. Surface meltwater drains via moulins, crevasses, tunnels.
- Ice sheets drive katabatic winds; ice cores record climate back nearly 1 million years.
- For most of the 20th century the ice sheets were nearly in balance; the 21st century changed this.
- No Gt/yr loss rates given on these pages.
